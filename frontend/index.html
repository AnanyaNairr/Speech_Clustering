Acoustic Feature Clustering for Safe Voice Control

Individuals with motor disabilities rely on spoken commands like "yes", "no", "stop", and "go". When commands sound acoustically similar, voice systems confuse them—causing dangerous failures (such as executing "go" instead of "stop"). This tool groups spoken sounds by their acoustic features so designers can select commands that are easy for both computers and humans to tell apart.

Design Principle: Choose voice commands that belong to different acoustic clusters to maximize separation and eliminate accidental triggers.

💡 What are Audio Clips in this Project?

Each voice command is a 1-second .wav audio file sampled at 16,000 Hertz (16kHz mono). If a clip is shorter than 1 second, it is padded with silence; if longer, it is trimmed to exactly 1.0 second. This ensures uniform feature extraction across all commands.

💡 What are MFCCs and Spectral Features?

• MFCC (Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients): Mathematical descriptors of the human vocal tract shape. Like a "fingerprint" of vowel resonances.
• Spectral Centroid: The "center of mass" of sound frequencies. Higher values sound bright and sharp (like "s" or "t"); lower values sound warm and bassy (like "o" or "u").
• Zero-Crossing Rate (ZCR): How rapidly the audio waveform crosses zero. High ZCR indicates noisy, hiss-like fricatives ("s", "f", "sh").
• StandardScaler: Adjusts every feature to have a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1 so high-magnitude frequencies don't overwhelm subtle timbre differences.

🛡️ Limitations, Demographics & Responsible Accessibility Use

1. Volunteer Demographics & Healthy Speech Bias:

The Google Speech Commands dataset was contributed by volunteer English speakers in controlled acoustic environments. Variations in regional accents, age (children and elderly speakers), vocal fatigue, and background ambient noise vary significantly in real homes and assistive environments.

2. Under-representation of Dysarthric & Impaired Speech:

Crucially, individuals who experience severe motor disabilities (e.g. Cerebral Palsy, ALS, Stroke survivors) frequently present with dysarthria, vocal tremor, reduced breath support, or non-standard articulation. The target population who most urgently requires hands-free accessibility control is fundamentally under-represented in standard volunteer datasets. These acoustic clusters reflect healthy acoustic baselines and must not be assumed to match dysarthric speech patterns without personalized acoustic calibration.

3. Exploratory Nature of Unsupervised Clusters:

Unsupervised clustering identifies geometric proximities in mathematical feature space. They are exploratory diagnostic tools—not immutable natural categories. Assistive system designers must never ship a voice interface without rigorous user-in-the-loop validation, safety confirmation prompts for destructive actions, and customizable fallback inputs.

📖 Glossary & How This Works

Simple Analogy: Acoustic Fingerprints

Imagine every word you speak has a unique shape and color. A word like "stop" starts with a hiss and ends with an abrupt click (sharp and pointed), whereas "go" is smooth, deep and rounded. Our algorithms measure these acoustic dimensions so we can group similar-sounding words together automatically.

Key Technical Terms Explained Simply

  • MFCC (Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients): A compact 13-number summary of how sound energy is distributed across pitch bands, tuned to how the human ear perceives sound.
  • Spectral Centroid: The "center of gravity" of audio frequencies. Higher values sound bright, sharp or hiss-like; lower values sound deep and muffled.
  • Zero-Crossing Rate (ZCR): How many times per second the audio wave crosses zero. High ZCR detects whispery or fricative sounds like "s", "f", and "sh".
  • K-Means vs K-Medians: K-Means uses Euclidean (straight-line) distance and updates centers via averages. K-Medians uses Manhattan (grid-like cityblock) distance and updates centers via medians, making it more resistant to outlier background noises.
  • Silhouette Score: A grade from -1 to +1 showing whether each clip belongs more to its assigned group than to neighboring groups.
  • Davies-Bouldin Index: A ratio of how wide clusters are compared to how far apart they are. Lower scores mean better-separated clusters.
  • PCA (Principal Component Analysis): Compresses 45 acoustic measurements down into 2 or 3 master coordinates (PC1 and PC2) so we can visualize complex sound patterns on a flat screen.
  • Adjusted Rand Index (ARI): Measures whether restarting the clustering algorithm with different random starting points discovers the same clusters. An ARI near 1.0 indicates high stability.
